The True Romance of Old Hollywood

Written By    Jeremy Oberstein

The Safari Inn reflects Burbank’s unique identity, part studio town, part suburban community, part time capsule.

That identity did not emerge by accident. It was built during the San Fernando Valley’s transformation from farmland into the production capital of the entertainment industry, when places like the Safari Inn became woven into the everyday infrastructure of Hollywood itself.

Today, the glowing motel on Olive Avenue feels almost anachronistic. Surrounded by newer development and a changing entertainment economy, the Safari Inn remains one of the Valley’s last authentic roadside motor lodges, a surviving artifact from the era when the San Fernando Valley became synonymous with the business of making movies.

Built in the 1950s during Southern California’s postwar boom, the Safari Inn arrived at a pivotal moment in Valley history. Wartime industries had already reshaped Burbank through the rise of Lockheed and nearby aviation manufacturing, but by midcentury, the entertainment industry was rapidly cementing its dominance across the eastern San Fernando Valley. Warner Bros., Disney and NBC expanded operations nearby. Universal transformed from a movie ranch into a sprawling studio-tourism complex over the hill.
At the same time, the automobile reshaped Southern California life.

The Valley’s growing freeway network turned Olive Avenue into a corridor for tourists, studio workers and business travelers moving between Hollywood, downtown Los Angeles and the expanding suburbs of the San Fernando Valley. Motels sprang up to accommodate the endless flow of drivers arriving in a region increasingly defined by mobility and entertainment.
The Safari Inn was part of that boom.

Unlike grand old Hollywood hotels built for movie stars, the Safari Inn reflected the working-class side of the industry. This was the Los Angeles of assistant directors, prop builders, location scouts and exhausted crews grabbing a few hours of sleep between shoots. The motel’s exterior corridors, central pool and glowing neon sign embodied the rise of California’s roadside culture, when architecture itself was designed to catch the attention of passing motorists.
General manager Sam Patel, vice president of operations at AGA Essentials, which took over the Safari Inn in December 2025, sees that history as central to the property’s identity.

“The neon sign, right away, is a big eye-catcher because it’s one of the largest neon signs in California,” Patel said. “It catches people’s eyes because the design is so far from a regular hotel.”

In many ways, the motel’s design reflected the broader personality of midcentury Burbank itself: practical, optimistic and deeply tied to the entertainment industry.

The Safari Inn eventually became part of Hollywood mythology. Over the decades, more than 50 productions filmed there, according to Patel, including True Romance, which transformed the motel into a cult landmark for film fans drawn to its neon-lit noir atmosphere. The property also played a supporting actor role in Apollo 13 and Coach Carter.

But perhaps more important than what was filmed there is what the motel represented. For much of the late 20th century, Burbank functioned as a true company town for Hollywood. Studio employees filled nearby neighborhoods. Production trucks clogged side streets. Local businesses depended on the entertainment economy. The Valley’s identity became inseparable from the machinery of film and television production. That ecosystem now feels more fragile.

Production has steadily migrated away from Los Angeles in recent years as studios chase tax incentives and lower costs elsewhere. Entire generations of Valley residents who once worked steadily in film and television now face uncertainty. Soundstages that once operated around the clock sit quieter more often.

As Los Angeles changes, places like the Safari Inn take on greater symbolic weight.

“I think Safari Inn becomes Burbank’s identity,” Patel said. “Burbank is different because it has a strong sense of local character tied to the entertainment industry, old Hollywood studios and creative culture.”
He is right. The motel survives as a physical reminder of the Valley’s evolution from agricultural outpost to suburban production capital. In a region that frequently demolishes its own past, the Safari Inn still looks and feels like old Southern California.

Today, as new ownership takes on the challenge of invigorating the Safari Inn, the neon still glows. Palm trees still tower over the courtyard. Airplanes still descend overhead toward Burbank Airport just as they did generations ago.
And maybe that continuity matters more now because Los Angeles itself feels increasingly disconnected from the version of Hollywood that built the Valley’s identity in the first place.

There was a time when nearby streets routinely filled with camera trucks and catering tents. When local diners buzzed with production chatter after midnight. When motels like the Safari Inn served as unofficial annexes to the studio system.

Today, many of those productions happen somewhere else.
But the Safari Inn remains, stubbornly holding onto the atmosphere of a Los Angeles steeped in the noir and nostalgia of an industry stubbornly fighting for its life. It’s a place that exists as a reminder that before streaming platforms and tax incentives turned entertainment into a borderless industry, L.A. was an industry town. And much of that was right here in the San Fernando Valley.

“Having that feeling is the most valuable thing [about] the Safari Inn,” Patel said. “You stay there for that experience of being back in the past.”
